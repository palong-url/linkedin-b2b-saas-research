## 1. Overview
This playbook is for B2B SaaS founders and marketers who want to build
pipeline and audience through founder-led LinkedIn organic content,
without paid distribution. It's built from analyzing 50 posts across
10 practitioners with verifiable track records — not generic LinkedIn
"growth hacking" advice.

The core thesis, borrowed most explicitly from Kieran Flanagan's
framing of Herbert Simon's 1971 attention economics: AI has made
content supply near-infinite, which makes owned attention (a
following that recognizes you specifically) more valuable, not less.
This playbook is about building that owned attention deliberately —
choosing a channel, a voice, and a system, and sticking with it long
enough for compounding to work
(source: Kieran Flanagan, LinkedIn post, undated — link in
research/linkedin-posts/kieran-flanagan/posts.md).

Scope: this is written for a single founder or small marketing team,
not an enterprise marketing org with dedicated headcount for
LinkedIn.

## 2. Core Playbook / SOP

### 2.1 Start with your growth motion, not a content calendar
Before picking formats or cadence, decide what growth motion your
content needs to support: discovery-led (SEO/comparison-style
content), product-led (onboarding and use-case content), or
sales-led (the asset your sales team is actually asking for, like a
buyer guide or follow-up sequence). Pick the content priority that
matches your motion instead of copying a generic content calendar.
(source: Amanda Natividad, LinkedIn post, SparkToro)

### 2.2 Post as a person, not a company page
Personal profiles get roughly 8x the reach and 12x the engagement of
Company Pages on LinkedIn; a single post from a C-level exec can
reach more qualified buyers in 48 hours than a month of Company Page
posts. B2B buyers follow people, not logos.
(source: Richard van der Blom, LinkedIn post, Algorithm Insights
Report 2026)

### 2.3 Pick one channel and go deep before expanding
Don't spread thin across LinkedIn, YouTube, newsletter, and TikTok at
once. Pick one channel, go deep, and only then look for ways to stack
those wins into other channels.
(source: Dave Gerhardt, LinkedIn post, Exit Five)

### 2.4 Write short, plain, opinionated hooks
Open with a short, direct, often contrarian statement (5-10 words)
rather than a soft or hedged one. Vague, agreeable openers get
scrolled past; specific opinions repel the wrong audience fast and
pull the right audience in.
(source: Justin Welsh, LinkedIn posts — pattern observed across
multiple posts)

### 2.5 Build one core asset, then generate many hooks from it
Rather than creating a new idea for every post, take one substantial
asset (a guide, a framework, a report) and write multiple posts that
approach it from different angles or personal stories, all pointing
to the same core offer.
(source: Justin Welsh, LinkedIn posts — pattern observed across
multiple posts)

### 2.6 Use "saves" as your best reach signal, not just likes
A post with 10+ saves reaches a median of over 2,000% more people
than a post with zero saves. Write posts people want to bookmark and
revisit (frameworks, checklists, references), not just posts that get
a quick like.
(source: Richard van der Blom, LinkedIn post, Algorithm Insights
Report 2026)

### 2.7 Avoid "reshare with your own thoughts"
LinkedIn's algorithm treats reshare-with-comment as low-effort copied
content and suppresses its reach. If you want to amplify someone
else's post, comment directly on it or use an instant reshare instead
of a reshare-with-added-commentary.
(source: Richard van der Blom, LinkedIn post, Algorithm Insights
Report 2026)

### 2.8 Engage manually and consistently, especially early
Reply to comments yourself, run a standing "ask me anything" in your
own comments, and treat every reply as a potential relationship, not
just an engagement metric. One practitioner built to 366,000+
followers over 5 years with 3-4 posts/week and 1,000+ manual comment
replies per week, without automation.
(source: Jasmin Alić, LinkedIn post)

### 2.9 Use a simple content framework per post
Each post should map to one topic and one takeaway ("share"), show
real results or work ("showcase"), and make an offer without it
feeling like a pitch ("sell"). Rotate through all three rather than
only ever sharing or only ever pitching.
(source: Jasmin Alić, LinkedIn post — "Share, Showcase, Sell"
framework)

### 2.10 If you use AI in your writing process, ground it in your own
voice and judgment, not generic prompts
Practitioners who use AI successfully in content production do it by
feeding the model their own writing rules, transcripts, and
frameworks (e.g. training on a specific writing philosophy, or
building an internal knowledge base of team learnings) rather than
using generic prompts. AI amplifies an existing point of view; it
doesn't substitute for one.
(source: Dickie Bush, LinkedIn post; Kieran Flanagan, LinkedIn post)

*(See Section 3 for where this directly conflicts with other

## 3. Where Experts Disagree
*(Minimum 3 examples)*

### Disagreement 1: Should content production use AI tools?
- **Dickie Bush / Nicolas Cole / Kieran Flanagan recommend:** Use AI
  actively in the writing process. Dickie Bush trained an AI writing
  coach on David Ogilvy's rules and built "Claude Skills" that draft
  LinkedIn posts directly. Nicolas Cole, despite framing himself
  around writing craft, promotes a 30-day path to becoming a paid "AI
  LinkedIn Ghostwriter" using AI prompts and voice-matching tools.
  Kieran Flanagan frames "scaling AI workflows" as one of two core
  skills of a modern marketing leader.
- **Amanda Natividad / Dave Gerhardt recommend:** Be skeptical of
  AI-generated content. Amanda argues an LLM can execute an existing
  recipe but "can't taste" — it has no lived experience of the
  product, the customer call, or the failure, and the most defensible
  content comes from people who actually do the work. Dave Gerhardt
  directly criticizes the flood of AI-generated marketing content
  ("the Slop Machine") and argues good marketing requires craft and
  conviction, not automation.
- **My take:** I side closer to Amanda and Dave for the *substance*
  of a post (the opinion, the story, the specific claim), but closer
  to Dickie and Kieran for the *mechanics* (drafting, editing,
  repurposing). The distinction that matters is where the judgment
  comes from. AI trained on someone's own transcripts, rules, or
  frameworks (Dickie's Ogilvy-based coach, Kieran's internal
  knowledge base) is still encoding a specific human point of view —
  it's a production tool. AI used to generate the opinion itself,
  with no first-hand input, is what produces the generic "it's not
  this, it's that" content Amanda is criticizing. So: AI for
  drafting and editing, never for sourcing the actual insight.

### Disagreement 2: Should engagement and outreach be automated?
- **Jasmin Alić recommends:** Never automate. She attributes 366,000+
  followers over 5 years directly to never-once automating activity,
  and replying to 1,000+ comments per week manually herself.
- **Dickie Bush recommends:** Build systems and AI tools to handle
  parts of the content and engagement workflow at scale (post
  drafting, writing audits) so a single person can operate like a
  team.
- **My take:** I take Jasmin's side, but only for the *engagement*
  half, not the *drafting* half. Manual replies are themselves the
  product for a personal brand — they're what a reader means when
  they say a creator is "accessible." Automating replies undermines
  the exact thing that's building the audience. But this is
  effort-intensive in a way the sources don't fully disclose: Jasmin
  Alić's system requires effectively a part-time job's worth of
  comment-replying every week, which is a real cost most early-stage
  founders underestimate (see Section 6).

### Disagreement 3: Follow the algorithm's data, or trust your own instinct?
- **Richard van der Blom recommends:** A data-driven, close-to-
  prescriptive approach — post formats, reshare behaviors, and
  posting patterns should be chosen based on what his Algorithm
  Insights Report shows the algorithm rewards (e.g. avoiding
  reshare-with-comment, prioritizing saves).
- **Dave Gerhardt recommends:** The opposite instinct — "Don't follow
  best practices. Use your brain. Be creative." His argument is that
  good marketing is often hard to measure and doesn't fit a formula,
  and treating content like a checklist produces generic output.
- **My take:** I take Richard's side for anything structural
  (format, reshare behavior, posting cadence) because that's
  measurable and his data is the most rigorous source in this
  research set. I take Dave's side for anything about voice, story,
  and point of view, because no algorithm report can tell you what's
  worth saying. In practice this means: use van der Blom's data to
  decide *how* to post, and ignore it when deciding *what* to say.

## 4. What I Rejected and Why

### Rejected idea 1: Using Matt Ragland and Chris Walker as primary
sources for LinkedIn content tactics
Both were selected in `sources.md` on the strength of their broader
business credibility (Matt Ragland's content-to-revenue systems,
Chris Walker's Refine Labs/Passetto case study). But across 5 sampled
posts each, neither's actual LinkedIn content addresses LinkedIn
posting tactics, formats, or algorithm behavior directly. Matt
Ragland's posts are about coaching pricing, AI prompting workflow,
and email list segmentation. Chris Walker's posts are about toxic
workplaces, the future of knowledge work, and Series A fundraising
honesty. I rejected using either as a source for tactical
recommendations in Section 2, because doing so would mean citing
their general credibility rather than evidence actually present in
their content — which is exactly the kind of unearned citation this
playbook is supposed to avoid.

### Rejected idea 2: Optimizing purely for van der Blom's algorithm
data as a complete strategy
Richard van der Blom's data (8x reach for personal profiles, reach
penalties for reshare-with-comment, etc.) is the most rigorous
quantitative source in this research. It would be easy to build the
entire playbook around his findings. I rejected doing that because
his data describes what the algorithm currently rewards, not what
builds a durable audience — those aren't guaranteed to be the same
thing, and algorithm-optimized content is also the fastest thing to
go stale when LinkedIn changes its ranking model. I used his data for
structural, format-level decisions (Section 2.2, 2.6, 2.7) but
deliberately did not let it define voice, topic choice, or cadence
philosophy.

### Rejected idea 3: Jasmin Alić's "never automate anything" as a
blanket rule
Jasmin's growth is real and well-documented, but "never automate" was
tested at her scale (366K followers, apparently full-time focus on
this). I rejected presenting this as a universal rule for a
resource-constrained founder, because the same manual-reply system
that builds trust at her scale may not be feasible for someone
running a company and doing LinkedIn as one part of their job. I kept
the *principle* (manual replies build trust) but rejected the
absolute framing ("never-once").

## 5. My Original Ideas
*(Minimum 1 idea NOT found in sources)*

### Original idea 1: "Where I disagree" as a recurring content format
None of the 10 experts researched write this way: every single one of
them posts as if their approach is simply correct, with no visible
acknowledgment that other credible practitioners recommend the
opposite. Richard van der Blom never mentions that Dave Gerhardt
explicitly rejects "best practices" thinking. Jasmin Alić never
addresses that Dickie Bush automates parts of the exact workflow she
says should never be automated. Each voice is internally consistent
but the field as a whole is not — and nobody in this research set
seems to be posting *about* that.

**The idea:** build a recurring content format — roughly one post
every few weeks — structured explicitly as "Expert A says X, Expert B
says Y, here's which one I'd bet on and why." Not a neutral summary;
a real, named disagreement with a real, argued stance.

**Why it could work:**
1. **It's a structurally different hook than anything in this
   research.** Every pattern found in Section 2 (contrarian openers,
   listicles, share/showcase/sell) is a *format*. This is closer to a
   *position* — it doesn't compete with those formats, it can be
   written using them (e.g. a contrarian Justin-Welsh-style opener:
   "Everyone tells you to follow the algorithm data. I don't. Here's
   why.").
2. **It converts research effort directly into content**, which
   matters for a solo operator or small team — the exact research
   process this playbook came from (reading conflicting expert
   advice, deciding what to believe) *is* the content, rather than
   being separate overhead before the content.
3. **It's naturally differentiated and hard to copy at scale**,
   because it requires actually having read multiple credible,
   conflicting sources closely enough to represent both sides fairly
   — something the AI-generated "10x your content" posts Amanda
   Natividad criticizes (Section 3, Disagreement 1) structurally
   cannot do well, since it requires a judgment call, not a synthesis
   of existing takes.
4. **It plausibly builds trust faster than one-sided advice.**
   Openly arguing against a credible source, by name, signals the
   writer has actually weighed alternatives — which is a more
   specific trust signal than generic "here's what works" advice,
   and harder to fake.

This is untested — it's the highest-uncertainty recommendation in
this playbook precisely because no source data supports or refutes
it. It should be piloted, not assumed (see Section 6).

## 6. Weaknesses of This Playbook

- **Small, non-random sample.** 5 posts per expert (50 total) is
  enough to spot patterns but not enough to be confident those
  patterns are each expert's actual dominant strategy rather than
  whatever posts happened to be recent or visible when collected.

- **Survivorship bias.** All 10 experts were chosen because they
  succeeded. Nothing in this research shows what similar tactics look
  like when they fail, so the playbook can't distinguish "this tactic
  works" from "this person would have succeeded regardless of
  tactic."

- **Gap between why an expert was selected and what their content
  actually shows.** For at least 3 of 10 experts (Matt Ragland, Chris
  Walker, and Nicolas Cole's "human beats AI" positioning), the
  reasoning in `sources.md` didn't hold up once individual posts were
  reviewed. I corrected for this where I caught it, but it raises the
  possibility of similar mismatches I didn't catch elsewhere in the
  research.

- **Self-reported data.** Richard van der Blom's statistics (8x
  reach, 2,000%+ save-driven reach, etc.) come from a report he
  produces and sells access to. The numbers may well be accurate —
  he has real scale and a multi-year track record — but there's no
  independent replication of them in this research.

- **No dates on collected posts.** Every citation in this playbook
  needs a real publish date added before it meets the source-citation
  requirement. Until that's done, there's no way to check whether the
  advice reflects LinkedIn's current algorithm or an older one — this
  matters a lot given how much Richard van der Blom's own data shows
  the algorithm has changed over time.

- **No original test.** Nothing in this playbook has been run against
  a real account. It's a synthesis of what 10 people say and do, not
  a validated result. Recommendation 2.9 (Share/Showcase/Sell) and
  2.5 (one asset, many hooks) are the easiest to pilot cheaply; I'd
  test those first before trusting the rest.

- **Narrow context.** All 10 experts operate in a US/English-language,
  personal-brand-heavy corner of B2B marketing. None of this was
  tested for non-English markets, more conservative/regulated B2B
  industries (e.g. healthcare, finance), or team-based (vs. founder-
  led) content strategies.

## 7. Who I Would NOT Recommend Following, and Why

**Chris Walker** — Selected on the strength of building Refine Labs
and Passetto using LinkedIn as a primary acquisition channel, which
is a genuinely strong credential. But the actual content sampled
doesn't demonstrate the mechanism behind that success. His posts are
generic thought-leadership (toxic workplace stats, the future of
knowledge work, a "radically honest" take on Series A fundraising)
that could be written by almost any operator-turned-commentator —
there's no visible thread back to the specific demand-gen or
dark-social tactics his reasoning in `sources.md` credits him for. If
someone followed him specifically to learn LinkedIn-to-pipeline
tactics, the content itself wouldn't teach them that.

**Matt Ragland** — Same underlying issue. His content is genuinely
useful for creator monetization, pricing psychology, and email
segmentation, but that's a different discipline from LinkedIn organic
content strategy. Recommending him under this topic specifically
would be recommending him for something his visible content doesn't
actually cover.

Neither of these is a case of bad advice — it's a case of the
research reasoning (built on their broader reputation and business
outcomes) not matching what a reader would actually get from
following their day-to-day content on this specific topic. Worth
flagging as a methodology lesson for future research: reputation and
outcome credentials are necessary but not sufficient — the content
itself has to be checked before recommending someone for a specific,
narrow use case.
